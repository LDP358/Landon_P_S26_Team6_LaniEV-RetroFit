### Objective
This project's primary customer is Lani Solar, LLC (LaniEV). LaniEV has developed a standardized retrofit kit for existing models of electric Club Car golf cars and is in the process of developing a universal conversion kit for low-speed electric vehicles (LSVs), personal transport vehicles (PVTs),  and golf cars. The Club Car retrofit kit and the universal conversion kits share the function of transforming legacy lead-acid battery systems to LiFePO₄ (Lithium Iron Phosphate) battery banks integrated with self-charging roof solar panels. The goal is to commercialize the conversion kits for entire fleet conversions. Secondary stakeholders include Tennessee Technological University (TTU), as the owner of the control golf car used for the project, and as the ensuring agency for documented safety, reliability, and performance data. 

Legacy golf cars continue to utilize lead-acid battery systems due to their well-documented electrical performance and broad availability. These systems present constraints such as low energy density, volume constraints, short lifecycle, load limits, maintenance requirements, and environmental concerns. These limitations increase cost and decrease sustainability for large-scale applications. Alternative power systems, such as LiFePO₄ batteries integrated with solar charging, could potentially address the limitations inherent in the lead-acid batteries. The growing interest in alternative systems demands formal documentation for conversion processes. The lack of validation prevents large-scale migration of LSV, PVT, and golf car platforms to alternative energy systems.
 
The primary objective of this project is to develop a universal solar panel attachment kit. The attachment kit is intended to be used on any of the three leading golf car manufacturers. This project will also address the lack of technical validation through meticulous examination and documentation of the retrofit process, and will also establish a validated framework for implementing the standardized retrofit kit. The development includes initial state characterization of the existing lead-acid system, formalization of the conversion methodology, post-conversion performance evaluation, and a quantified analysis of environmental and economic impact. The Tennessee Tech University Club Car will serve as the control platform for this process. Baseline electrical and functional characteristics of the legacy system shall be documented prior to conversion. Following installation of the LiFePO₄ battery and solar charging systems, the vehicle shall undergo structured testing to quantify energy efficiency, power delivery, charging behavior, and operational performance. The resulting documentation shall provide the necessary metrics required for the retrofit kit to be commercialized.

## **Power Solutions for a Solar Powered Golf Car**
There are multiple solutions to powering a golf car. In this section, various potential solutions are hypothesized, design considerations are discussed, and factors influencing the selection of a solution are outlined. The chosen solution is then identified with justifications for its selection.

**Gas:** Gas powered golf cars are fueled by gasoline using a combustion engine. While gas powered cars are powerful and have a long range, they produce direct emissions, require regular maintenance, and are louder than electric alternatives, making them less suitable for the environmentally conscious goals of this project.

**Traditional Electric:** Traditional electric golf cars are the most popular cars in the golfing industry because they are easy to recharge and low maintenance. The electric golf car runs on a battery pack, typically lead-acid or lithium-ion, that is charged by plugging into a standard electrical outlet. They produce zero direct emissions and are quieter than gas powered golf cars, making them the preferred choice for most golf courses. However, traditional electric golf cars are dependent on the power grid for charging, which limits their sustainability and independence from conventional energy sources.

**Renewable:** Renewable energy powered golf cars use natural energy sources to charge or directly power the car. The most practical renewable solution for a golf car is solar energy, delivered through solar panels mounted on the roof of the car. Solar panels convert sunlight into electricity using the photovoltaic effect, which is then used to charge the battery pack and power the car. This solution eliminates dependence on the power grid, produces zero direct emissions, and has lower long term operating costs compared to both gas and traditional electric options.
There are different kinds of solar panels in the market today. The most common are monocrystalline, polycrystalline, and thin-film solar panels. Each type was evaluated based on the following design considerations: dimensions, weight, cost, lifespan, efficiency, and durability. Additionally, each panel type was assessed based on the following influencing factors: where it is produced, cost, and environmental impact.

### **Monocrystalline:** 
The first monocrystalline solar cell was invented at Bell Labs by Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson with a 6% efficiency rate. This efficiency rate continued to grow over the years, reaching 17-24% in modern panels. Monocrystalline solar cells are made of a single-crystal silicon slice grown using the Czochralski process.
#### **Design Considerations:**
**Dimensions:** A standard 400W monocrystalline panel measures approximately 79 x 39 inches.
    
**Weight**: A typical 400W panel weighs around 44-50 lbs, adding significant weight to the golf car which could affect performance and handling.

**Lifespan:** Monocrystalline panels have a lifespan of 25-40 years, the longest of the three panel types.

**Efficiency:** With an efficiency rate of 17-24%, monocrystalline panels are the most efficient option.

**Durability:** Monocrystalline panels are highly durable and perform well in a variety of weather conditions.

#### **Influencing Factors:**

**Where it is Produced:** Monocrystalline panels are primarily produced in China, with the largest manufacturers being LONGi Green Energy, JinkoSolar, and Trina Solar. This concentration of production in a single country can affect supply chain reliability and lead times.

**Cost:** At $280-$440 for a typical 400W panel, monocrystalline panels are the most expensive option upfront. However, their longer lifespan and higher efficiency can offset this cost over time, making them a strong long term investment.

**Environmental Impact:** The manufacturing process for monocrystalline panels is energy intensive, requiring high temperatures and extremely pure silicon. However, once installed, they produce clean energy with zero direct emissions and have the longest lifespan of the three types, reducing the frequency of replacement and waste.

### **Polycrystalline:** 
Polycrystalline solar panels were developed in 1981 as a cheaper alternative to monocrystalline panels. Instead of using a single crystal of silicon, polycrystalline panels are made by melting multiple silicon fragments together, which gives them their characteristic blue, speckled appearance.
#### **Design Considerations:**

**Dimensions:** A standard 400W polycrystalline panel measures approximately 79 x 39 inches.

**Weight:** A typical 400W polycrystalline panel weighs around 44-50 lbs, similar to monocrystalline panels

**Lifespan:** Polycrystalline panels have a lifespan of 25-35 years, slightly shorter than monocrystalline but still very long.

**Efficiency:** With an efficiency rate of 15-17%, polycrystalline panels are less efficient than monocrystalline.

**Durability:** Polycrystalline panels are durable and weather resistant, though they tend to perform slightly worse than monocrystalline in high heat conditions

#### **Influencing Factors:**

**Where it is Produced:** Like monocrystalline panels, polycrystalline panels are predominantly manufactured in China, with some production in other Asian countries such as Malaysia and Vietnam. This makes them subject to similar supply chain considerations as monocrystalline panels.

**Cost:** At $200-$320 for a typical 400W panel, polycrystalline panels are the most affordable silicon based option, making them attractive for projects with tighter budgets. However their lower efficiency means more panels may be needed to achieve the same power output

**Environmental Impact:** Polycrystalline panels have a slightly lower environmental impact during manufacturing compared to monocrystalline because the crystal growth process is simpler and less energy intensive. They still produce clean energy during operation, though their shorter lifespan means they may need to be replaced sooner, generating more waste over time

### **Thin-Film (CIGS):**
CIGS (Copper Indium Gallium Selenide) takes a different approach to solar technology compared to silicon-based panels. Instead of slicing wafers from a crystal, the semiconductor material is deposited directly onto a substrate in ultra-thin layers. First developed in 1974, CIGS panels are the fastest to produce of the three types, taking only 1-2 days to manufacture.

#### **Design Considerations:**

**Dimensions:** CIGS panels are more flexible in terms of form factor and can be made in custom sizes.

**Weight:** CIGS thin-film panels are significantly lighter than silicon based panels, typically weighing around 5-7 lbs per panel.

**Lifespan:** CIGS panels have a shorter lifespan of 10-20 years compared to silicon based panels.

**Efficiency:** With an efficiency rate of 12-14%, CIGS panels are the least efficient of the three options.

**Durability:** CIGS panels perform well in high temperatures and low light conditions, making them suitable for hot climates, but they are generally considered less durable than silicon based panels over the long term.

#### **Influencing Factors:**

**Where it is Produced:** CIGS panels are produced by a more geographically diverse set of manufacturers compared to silicon based panels, with notable producers in the 
United States, Germany, and Japan. This wider distribution of production can offer more supply chain flexibility and shorter lead times depending on location

**Cost:** At $240-$400 for a typical 400W panel, CIGS panels fall in the middle of the price range. However, their lower efficiency and shorter lifespan make them less cost effective over the long term compared to the silicon based alternatives

**Environmental Impact:** CIGS panels raise more environmental concerns than silicon based panels due to the use of indium and gallium, which are rare materials, and the presence of a small amount of cadmium in some variants, which is toxic. Their manufacturing process is less energy intensive than silicon panels, but the use of these rare and potentially hazardous materials is an important consideration for an environmentally focused project

### **FLA (Flooded Lead-Acid):**
The lead-acid battery was invented by French physician Gaston Planté in 1859. At the end of the 19th century electric cars appeared on the roads powered mostly by lead-acid batteries. The late 1960s saw improvements with casings making them thinner and lighting providing the use of more active material at no sacrifice of weight or volume. Despite being over 165 years old, FLA batteries remain widely used in golf carts due to their low cost and reliability. Each battery cell contains a series of lead dioxide (PbO2) plates as the positive electrode and sponge lead (Pb) plates as the negative electrode, submerged in an electrolyte solution. That electrolyte is a 35% sulfuric acid and 65% water solution which allows for the chemical reaction needed to release electrons. Lead-acid batteries also have a high recycling rate around 98%, this recycling rate alleviate concerns about the toxicity of their materials, and the environmental impact.

### **AGM (Absorbed Glass Mat):**
AGM technology was originally invented in 1972. AGM batteries were initially used in military aircraft because they were powerful, durable, and safe. They were later used as backup batteries for call and data centers, and eventually found their way into golf carts, marine vessels, RVs, and consumer vehicles. AGM batteries share the same basic chemistry as FLA batteries, with the difference being instead of a liquid the electrolyte solution is absorbed into mats of finely spun glass fibers. The mats soak up the solution like a sponge and are then sandwiched between the lead plates, creating a stable connection between the electrolyte and the plates.

### **Nickel-Cadmium (NiCd):**
NiCd batteries were invented by Swedish chemist Waldemar Jungner in 1899 as an alternative to lead-acid batteries. They are constructed from four key components a nickel oxyhydroxide cathode, a metallic cadmium anode, a 30% potassium hydroxide electrolyte solution, and a porous polymer separator between the plates. During discharge, the cadmium anode is oxidized and the nickel oxyhydroxide cathode is reduced, releasing electrical energy. During charging, the reaction is completely reversed, restoring the battery to its original state.

technology to be used. This would allow the world to be more exposed, in a positive way, to both solar and electric vehicle technologies. Intellectual innovations regarding
both solar and electric vehicle technologies would follow, allowing for improved designs and products in these areas of study. 

Environmental Impacts: A solar powered conversion kit would have both positive and negative impacts on the environment. Positives include reduced reliance on grid charging stations
for battery powered electric vehicles. Battery life would also be extended for such vehicles, assuming ideal weather conditions. Carbon emissions on a local scale would be reduced, as there would be less gas powered vehicles in use, as well. Negative environmental impacts would include the emissions created from the increased production of the solar panels in use by such retrofit kits. Extracting materials necessary for the production of these conversion kits would also create increased emissions on the environment. End of life disposal of batteries being used by these solar powered vehicles is a concern. It is paramount that used batteries be disposed of in a proper and professional manner. 

Economic Impacts: Converting an already existing battery powered golf car to being solar powered would be cheaper than the alternative of purchasing an entirely new golf car. For companies looking to mass-convert existing golf cars, it would also be an efficient and cost-effective way to convert large amounts of golf cars in a short period of time. 
Such a retrofit kit would also create a large amount of jobs, as there would be many more tasks that would need to be fulfilled to achieve the successful implementation of these kits. These jobs would appear all over the economy. Jobs would be created to gather the resources necessary for the production of these retrofit kits, as well as jobs in manufacturing, in order to create more things that the kit would possess, such as solar panels, solar-rechargeable batteries, arms to hold said solar panel, and any extra wiring needed for the implementation of the new battery. There would also be an increase in skilled labor, as jobs must be created to both install and maintain these retrofit kits.

Public Safety Impacts: Safety is of the utmost importance in a project such as this, that requires potentially fatal amounts of electricity to operate. All constraints and
specifications listed in the ***Fully Formulated Problem*** section must be followed to ensure the safety of all involved in the design, implementation, and consumption process of this retrofit kit.

Public Health Impacts: Carbon emissions would be reduced on a local scale by a considerable amount for consumers looking to convert fleets of golf cars into being solar powered. These reduced emissions would have a positive effect on the community, creating cleaner air for inhabitants. On a cognitive level, the development of a retrofit kit could potentially inspire increased research and innovations on technologies involved in the retrofit kit system.
